What is real

  • The wiring. 236 projection neurons, 3,741 Kenyon cells, 97 output neurons and 332 dopamine neurons, with 78,937 connections between them, taken from the MaleCNS v1.0 connectome. Every connection's strength is its measured synapse count.
  • Which outputs mean approach and which mean avoid. It follows each output neuron's chemical transmitter, using the rule found in fly experiments: glutamate outputs drive avoidance, the others approach.
  • Where dopamine lands. 69% of the reward neurons' synapses fall on avoid outputs and 91% of the punishment neurons' synapses fall on approach outputs. That measured pattern is what makes a win pull the fly towards a choice and a loss push it away.

What I made up

  • The cards. Flies smell, they do not read. Each situation plus each choice is turned into a made-up smell: a pattern across the fly's 57 strongest smell channels.
  • The neurons are simplified to activity levels, with no spikes. Only the most strongly driven 5% of Kenyon cells stay active, standing in for the neuron that keeps them quiet in a real fly.
  • The learning rule follows the textbook picture of this circuit (dopamine weakens the synapses that were just active), but its exact form and numbers are my choices.
  • No real fly was involved, and this is not a claim that flies can play cards.

Learning depends on dopamine reaching the right outputs. It does not depend on exactly which smell channel reaches which Kenyon cell: scrambling that wiring changes nothing, which fits what biologists found in real flies, where those connections look close to random.

    Rows are how good the fly's cards are compared with an average hand against the players still in. Columns are the street, and whether the fly can check for free, faces one bet, or faces a raise. A sensible player raises at the top, folds at the bottom when it costs something, and gets more careful when raised.

    What is real

    • Each fly's brain. All five run the same mushroom body circuit as the blackjack fly, with the same measured wiring and dopamine. Each fly has its own copy and learns only from its own wins and losses, so they drift apart.
    • The learning. As in blackjack, every choice a fly made in a hand is paired with the chips it won or lost, delivered as reward or punishment dopamine.

    What I made up

    • The strength smell. A fly cannot read cards or count outs. The page deals out the rest of the hand a few hundred times to see how often the fly's cards would win, and gives the fly that as part of the smell, sorted into ten steps from hopeless to a monster. What the fly learns is what to do about each smell.
    • The looks. Canton-S, white, yellow, ebony and Curly are real lab strains, but here they are only costumes: all five flies have the same wiring.
    • Limit rules keep it to three choices. Stacks are topped up to 100 when they drop below 50, and winnings above 250 are banked, so nobody goes all in and there are no side pots. The "Overall" column counts every chip.
    • How loud the cards are in the smell, and how often a fly tries something other than its favourite choice, are my settings. Without enough trying, a fly that loses a few early calls stops calling for good.
